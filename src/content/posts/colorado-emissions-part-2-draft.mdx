Part 1 of this series covered the sector that's actually working, electricity, and the sector that's stuck, transportation. Part 2 is the weird one, covering a sector that's quietly doing better than almost anyone gives it credit for, along with two sectors where the fight over progress is either just getting won in court or hasn't really started at all.

## Oil and Gas

If you asked most Coloradans which industry is dragging down our climate goals, oil and gas is probably the first answer, but the data tells a more complicated story than that. Emissions from natural gas and petroleum systems are down 24% since 2005, and the story behind that number is one of the more consistent regulatory efforts the state has ever run.

Colorado was the first state in the country to set methane targets specifically for oil and gas, back in February 2014, and since then the Air Quality Control Commission has gone back and updated Regulation 7 almost every single year, closing exemptions and tightening leak detection requirements as the technology to measure emissions got better. In 2023 we became the first state to require an intensity verification rule, meaning operators actually have to prove their reported emissions match what independent measurement finds. In December 2024 the state adopted the first-of-its-kind midstream rule targeting compressor stations and processing plants, and this past February the AQCC updated Regulation 7 again to align with the federal EPA methane rule from 2024.

The results are showing up in the data that matters most, which is satellite measurement rather than self-reporting. GOSAT satellite data confirmed that methane emissions and intensity actually dropped as these rules took effect, which is a much better sanity check than trusting what companies report on their own.

I don't want to oversell this though. A Capital & Main investigation in February found evidence that operators may still be underreporting their actual emissions compared to what aerial and satellite monitoring shows, so the trend is real but the numbers going into that trend line are probably still a little rosy. Even with that caveat, oil and gas is proof that a decade of consistent, iterative regulation actually moves the needle, using the same formula that worked for electricity: clear targets, an agency that keeps updating the rules as it learns more, and enforcement that isn't just symbolic.

## Buildings

Buildings are basically flat since 2005, with emissions coming almost entirely from natural gas used for heating, water heating, and cooking, and until very recently the policy tools to change that were weak and mostly voluntary.

Denver has been trying to force the issue since 2021, when the city council started passing rules requiring new commercial buildings to skip fossil fuel heating and water heating equipment. Predictably, the building industry sued, with the Restaurant Law Center and the National Association of Home Builders arguing in 2024 that Denver had no authority to do this because federal law, specifically a 1975 statute called the Energy Policy and Conservation Act, preempts states and cities from regulating appliance energy efficiency.

On August 7, Denver won, when a federal judge dismissed the case with prejudice, ruling that Denver's ordinance regulates which classes of appliances can be installed rather than the efficiency of a specific appliance, so it doesn't trigger federal preemption. This is a genuinely big deal, and it clears the way for Denver's next round of requirements, which force water heaters and boilers to go electric when they're replaced anyway. The city says the policy is already working, with three times as many all-electric buildings permitted in 2025 compared to the year before.

I'd love to end the buildings section there, but three months from now Colorado voters get to potentially undo all of it.

Initiative 177, deceptively called the "Right to Natural Gas," made the November ballot after a dark money group called Advance Colorado collected enough signatures. It's a two sentence constitutional amendment that says consumers have a right to buy natural gas for cooking and heating, and distributors have a right to sell it, with no definitions and no exceptions written in. If it passes with the 55% supermajority required for constitutional amendments, it could force Denver, and every other city with an electrification code, to reverse course entirely, undoing the legal win from days before with a single election.

This also blows up a truce that industry, environmental groups, and state lawmakers struck back in 2024, which was supposed to keep both sides off the ballot until 2028. House Democrats tried to pass guardrails around the initiative this spring to limit how broadly it could be implemented, but Republicans ran out the legislative clock on the last day of session and the bill died.

Buildings, in other words, just won an actual legal fight for the first time in years, and whether that win survives November is genuinely unclear.

## Agriculture

If oil and gas is the pleasant surprise and buildings is the ongoing fight, agriculture is the sector nobody's really fighting for at all. Emissions here are up 5% since 2005, driven almost entirely by livestock, which is up 23% as Colorado's cattle and dairy herds have grown.

The state created an Agricultural Drought and Climate Resilience Office in 2023, and CSU runs a solid research program called AgNext that's studying methane reduction in cattle, which is genuinely useful work. But research programs aren't policy, and Colorado has no mandatory emissions target for agriculture, nothing like California's SB 1383, which set an actual statutory methane reduction goal for dairy and livestock back in 2016.

Every single emissions projection scenario in the state's own inventory, including the most aggressive one modeling every policy currently being discussed, has agriculture emissions ten percent above 2005 levels by 2050, meaning not one modeled path gets this sector to reduce. That should tell you something about how seriously it's being treated.

## What this all adds up to

Two posts into this series, the pattern keeps repeating. Electricity worked because the state built a real regulatory structure and kept it updated for over a decade, and oil and gas worked for the exact same reason. Transportation and buildings both have the political will on paper but keep running into either weak enforcement or outright legal warfare, while agriculture doesn't even have a fight happening, quietly getting worse while everyone focuses somewhere else.

If Colorado wants to close the gap on its climate targets, the lesson from the sectors that actually worked isn't complicated. Set a real target, write a rule with teeth, and keep updating it as you learn more. We've done this twice now, and the question is whether we do it for the sectors we haven't gotten around to yet, or whether a ballot measure in November erases the one place we just made real progress.
